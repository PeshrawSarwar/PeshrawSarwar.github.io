From a Curious Child to a Gen Z Navigating the Corporate Rollercoaster

Growing up with technology, finding my direction, and learning that there’s always more to learn.

A personal retrospective. This essay reflects an earlier stage of my career; the experiences and wording below are preserved as written.

Where do we go from here?

Growing up as a Gen Z, the question of what to do after finishing university was something we often asked ourselves. Should we work independently? Join a small or large company? Pursue a master’s degree? We are the generation born and raised with technology and the internet, starting from desktop computers to smartphones, social media platforms, and online games like Farmville and Pool Live Tour. Along the way, I learned to jailbreak iOS devices and root Android phones, and later dove deeper into programming, software development, cybersecurity, and AI.

Perhaps we were fortunate to be born in an era of rapid technological advancements, where inventions and innovations flourished in just a couple of decades—and continue to grow.

It started with curiosity

My interest in technology began early. I had an eagerness to learn since childhood, starting with reading newspapers like Zansti Sardam and then moving to computers. My first experience with a computer was in 2005 when I was only 4 years old. I vividly remember using programs like Office 2003, Paint, Photoshop 2007 (the version with the eye icon), VLC Media Player, and playing games. Back then, I noticed the Java logo on some programs, but I had no idea what it was or why it was there. Those early impressions shaped my passion for technology and set me on the path I’m on today.

In 2013, I bought my first iPod Touch 5th Gen with the primary goal of playing games. However, once I got it, I wanted to explore more. I learned how to jailbreak it and install Cydia on iOS 6. I explored various tweaks from repositories like Big Boss, Hack Your iPhone, and Karwan BK to hack mobile games, customize my iPod, and try other tricks. Today, many of those features are now built into iOS itself, showing how Apple gradually incorporated the customizations we once needed Cydia for.

Learning how much there is to learn

Starting my undergraduate degree in IT gave me the opportunity to dive deep into various areas of technology. I realized that, despite my initial knowledge, I still had much to learn. I began studying subjects like Programming, Web Development, DBMS, Networks, Cybersecurity, Logics, Calculus, IoT, AI, and ML. Each of these topics requires years of learning and hands-on practice to master.

As someone who struggled with imposter syndrome, I wanted at least a general understanding of all these areas. While this approach had both pros and cons—it was time-consuming and prevented me from focusing on one specific field—it gave me a broad view of how technology works. Ultimately, it allowed me to make a more informed decision about which path to follow.

A turning point: learning by building

Then, COVID-19 hit during my second year of university. The lockdowns gave us several months at home, and I decided to use this time to explore web development and networking. I learned PHP for backend development, working with MySQL for database management, and studied networking topics as I prepared for my CCNA. Alongside that, I learned about system administration and cybersecurity, which solidified my passion for the field.

The lockdown became a turning point in my journey, allowing me to deepen my knowledge and apply what I had learned.

During this time, my friends and I decided to create management systems to apply what we had learned. Though we were inexperienced, we built several projects—some successful and others failures—but each was a valuable lesson. Looking back, we would be embarrassed by our early work, but those experiences laid the foundation for the knowledge and skills I developed later.

I also wanted formal training, so I joined the first WeCode bootcamp for UI/UX, organized by the Rwanga Foundation. This bootcamp introduced me to topics like user experience, UI design principles, and creating systems with real value. After completing it, I earned the Google UI/UX and Google Cybersecurity Specialization certificates. My curiosity about cybersecurity grew, as I wanted to understand the intricacies of technology and systems. It became not just a professional interest but a hobby that challenged me to think critically and solve problems.

Finding growth through community

In addition to learning and working, I felt something was missing—an opportunity to grow and connect with others. Volunteering was the answer. I started volunteering at the American Corner in Sulaymaniyah, which turned out to be one of the best decisions I’ve ever made. The people and community there were exceptional, and the experience still impacts me today. I’m proud of my time with the organization.

I also had the privilege of participating as a delegate in Iraq’s first-ever National Model United Nations Association Conference (NMUNA). This historic event brought together young people from all over Iraq and was made possible by Soran University, the University of Central Florida, the U.S. Embassy in Iraq, and IREX Organization. The experience left a profound impact on me, and I’m forever grateful for the opportunity to meet such incredible individuals.

Stepping into the working world

As university came to an end, I faced the common dilemma: what to do next? The job market in Kurdistan is competitive, and securing a position often requires exceptional skills or strong connections. My friends and I sought internships, and eventually, I secured one at a local company. After two months, I chose a different path—working as a Software Developer at a company specializing in networking technologies. That experience helped me grow and gave me hands-on experience in the job market.

Shortly after, I transitioned to a larger company in the fields of Construction, Oil & Gas Trading, Hospitality, and General Trading as an Application Support Engineer. This was my first real exposure to the corporate world, working with people from diverse backgrounds, cultures, and nationalities. It made me realize how much I still had to learn about technology and systems.

The next step took me to the largest telecom company in the country, where I currently work as a Web & Application Expert. Moving between these positions wasn’t easy, but it was made possible by the background I had built and the professional connections I had cultivated.

Learning doesn’t end at graduation

In the corporate world, one of the most important soft skills is adaptability. You enter a diverse environment where people come from all walks of life. Things don’t always go your way, and you’ll encounter people with a superiority complex. However, you’ll also meet many great individuals who will help you along the way. The corporate world is like a university—if you choose to, you can learn something new every day and improve your skills. But if you don’t take the opportunity, you may get stuck in the same place.

Balancing the constant pressures of corporate life—meeting deadlines, collaborating with diverse teams, and maintaining a work-life balance—is essential. However, equally important is prioritizing self-development, personal growth, and career advancement. Without proper control and discipline, it’s easy to become overwhelmed. Surrounding yourself with like-minded individuals who share your vision and mindset can provide the support and motivation needed to navigate these challenges effectively.

To remain competitive in the fast-moving world of technology, you must adopt a growth mindset. Stay open to new knowledge, embrace opportunities, and commit to lifelong learning. If you're in your 20s and the corporate environment is new to you, don’t worry—it’s completely fine. Stepping outside your comfort zone is essential for growth.

As Werner Heisenberg once said, “Only a few know how much one must know to know how little one knows.”
